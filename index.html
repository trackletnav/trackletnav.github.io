TrackletNav

Real-world Topological Navigation with Long-Term Capability in Complex Outdoor Scenes

Anonymous Author(s)
SISREN Workshop @ CoRL 2026
Paper arXiv Video Code (coming soon)

TrackletNav splits a single teach video into short tracklets and pairs the local planner with a VLM global planner that notices and corrects its mistakes on long outdoor routes.

Abstract

Topological navigation enables long-horizon navigation in unknown environments by decomposing complex routes into short-range subgoal-reaching tasks. However, existing methods often struggle in complex urban environments due to two key challenges: perceptual ambiguity, where visually similar locations or directions lead to incorrect subgoal selection, and execution drift, where imperfect local navigation causes accumulated deviations from the planned trajectory. We identify these failure modes through real-world experiments and reproduce them in simulation to demonstrate their impact on long-horizon navigation.

To address these challenges, we propose TrackletNav, a framework that decomposes long-horizon routes into shorter topological navigation segments, termed tracklets. TrackletNav combines history-aware memory to disambiguate visually similar subgoals with tracklet-based decomposition and agent-guided correction to mitigate execution drift. Extensive real-world evaluations in complex urban environments demonstrate that TrackletNav reduces the average remaining distance to the goal by over 50% compared to state-of-the-art topological navigation methods. Our findings further highlight visual place recognition failures as a remaining bottleneck toward reliable long-horizon navigation.

Overview of TrackletNav. A Qwen3-VL global planner supervises the GuideNav local planner tracklet by tracklet: it reverses the robot when a place-recognition error sends it the wrong way, and corrects its trajectory whenever it stops partway through a tracklet.

Why Is Real-World Topological Navigation Hard?

A topological navigation system selects the next subgoal by matching the current view against image nodes with visual place recognition (VPR), and a goal-reaching policy drives toward it. Existing methods are mostly evaluated indoors or in outdoor scenes with distinctive landmarks. In complex urban scenes, we identify two failure modes through real-world experiments and reproduce them in simulation:

1. Perceptual ambiguity

In repetitive environments, distinct locations or directions look alike, so subgoal selection picks the wrong node, for example on a long tree-lined walk.

2. Execution drift

Uneven terrain, obstacle avoidance and other disturbances push the robot off the planned trajectory, and the errors accumulate over long routes.

Method

TrackletNav repeats a route from one teach video using only an RGB camera, with no odometry or GPS. Offline, it splits the route into short, one-way tracklets: at junctions found from the teach video, and at extra cuts inside long tracklets, placed so that look-alike keyframes fall into different tracklets. During navigation, only the keyframes of the active tracklet are candidates for localization.

Online, a local planner (GuideNav) localizes and drives within the active tracklet, while a VLM global planner (Qwen3-VL) checks that it follows the plan. A history-aware memory of motion captions and recent frames is compared with each tracklet's text summary and keyframes. With this, agent-guided correction reverses the robot when a place-recognition error has sent it the wrong way, and issues small corrective turns when the local planner stops partway. The framework is model-agnostic.

Results

Real-to-sim digital twins

Following Wanderland, we scan four real scenes with a LiDAR scanner and rebuild them as photorealistic 3D Gaussian splatting digital twins (routes of 64–156 m). Each simulated run adds the walking gait's camera motion, motion blur, and the G1's measured command delay, so real-world failures can be reproduced and every method runs many times under the same conditions.

Simulation routes. Top: 3DGS render of each digital twin. Bottom: the taught route on the occupancy grid of the scan (red circle: start, yellow star: goal).

Real-world experiments

We deploy TrackletNav on a Unitree G1 humanoid with whole-body control and a head-mounted RealSense D455 camera on a custom mount, and run 70 trials across one indoor and two outdoor scenes. Bipedal walking adds execution noise and motion blur. Outdoors, each run allows up to three human interventions.

Indoor

Walk down a hallway, turn, and stop facing the elevator door. All baselines work well here, on par with a VLA (LightNav) fine-tuned on this scene.

Long-horizon outdoor

A long urban walk from a square, along repetitive paved paths, to a red sculpture. TrackletNav succeeds far more often and ends much closer to the goal.

Success: TrackletNav 4/5, GuideNav 1/5, PixelLoop 0/5
Remaining distance: 13.2 m vs. 52.9 m and 79.7 m

Landmarkless park (limitation)

Every direction shows only trees and chairs, so place recognition fails and the text memory gives no guidance. VPR remains the bottleneck of the framework.

Success: TrackletNav 2/5, GuideNav 2/5, PixelLoop 3/5
